01 · Adoption programme and strategy

Organisation-wide AI adoption programme and strategy

COAR Global, 2025 to 2026. Delivered; measurement layer proposed.

A three-year AI adoption plan, written and owned end to end; an AI use policy written from a staff survey and adopted as the organisation-wide norm; all staff and all seven senior managers trained; a map and four patterns that tell managers where AI earns its place; a rollout discipline that starts at 100 percent review; a standing group that decides which uses go ahead.

At a glance

Problem

COAR Global is an international research organisation of about 180 staff, more than 300 people with its field data collectors, that carries out research for United Nations agencies, with teams in Syria, Haiti, Myanmar and the Middle East and North Africa.

By early 2026 its staff were already using AI at work, many from personal accounts, and the organisation had no shared rule on which tools were approved or what data could go into them. Its research material includes sensitive sources and identifiable recordings. The 2023 three-year IT plan had nothing to say about any of it, and nothing told a manager which of the ideas landing in the team were worth building.

The strategy in six layers

Sequence

What the survey found

The floor: five rules and three data classes

  • Five golden rules. AI drafts, you decide; you own what you submit; verify everything; protect the data on organisation-managed accounts; be open about AI use.
  • Two questions classify any task. Which data is being touched, and where is the output going. Green, Amber or Red; unsure means Amber. Verification does not move a task down a class; the data does.
  • Amber in five steps. Anonymise, approved tools only, verify the output, supervisor review, record it in the audit trail.
  • Approved tools with no-training guarantees on the organisation's data, licensed for everyone on the organisational account.

Where AI earns its place

Four patterns, four systems

Rollout discipline

  • Pilot with what exists. Existing tools and the organisation's own infrastructure first; the screening workflow runs for about USD 7 a month on it. Buy or build only after a pilot proves the hours.
  • No owner, no build. Every workflow has a named person who wants it and will use it; ownership of the screening tracker sits with the business development lead.
  • Review time is budgeted. For Amber work, time to review AI outputs is included in the level of effort, like any other step.
  • Rules in code, judgement in the model. Eligibility gates, thresholds and schedules live in code; the model categorises, summarises and explains.

Governance

The AI Policy and Practice Group has met every two weeks since July 2026 with the chief executive, the heads of research and the technology function. It decides in writing which new uses go ahead, hears the quality reviews of running workflows, settles build-or-buy questions (the automation platform stays self-hosted; the CRM is built on the ERP rather than bought), and receives vendor evaluations with a recommendation. Decision briefs to senior management carry options, risks and one recommendation. A second workshop, on practical day-to-day use, was agreed in September for the teams that asked for it.

Measuring maturity, proposed

Training

90-minute sessions were delivered remotely inside each team's standing meeting, so no new meeting was created: the survey's own findings first, then data classification before any prompt, prompt structure, hallucination case studies and a six-scenario quiz. Anyone who missed a session received the recording, the slides and the policy. Senior management had its own 60-minute edition, half hands-on and half direction: the map, the four patterns and a one-page manager handout.

Result

The policy is the organisation-wide norm. All staff are trained, and all seven members of senior management attended their session. Six workflows run in production under the plan, each in the quick-win quadrant and each with a named person who signs off. New AI uses are decided by a standing group and recorded. The survey results, attendance, participant feedback and session materials are all documented.

What was learned

  • Willingness was not the gap; confidence about data was. Opening each session with the survey's own findings, then making safe use concrete with Green, Amber and Red, is what made the policy stick without any authority over the research teams that adopted it.
  • Technology was never the blocker; method and ownership were. Ideas without a written method or a named owner did not reach production, whatever the tool. The map, the owner rule and the review ladder exist because of that.
  • Autonomy is earned by measurement. A workflow that starts at 100 percent review and steps down on evidence is accepted by the teams whose work it replaces; one that starts autonomous is not.

Role

Gerard Bou Faical, Head of Technology and AI Consultant, COAR Global and member of the senior management team, wrote the plan and the policy, designed and ran the survey, delivered every session, built the workflows that followed and convenes the group.
